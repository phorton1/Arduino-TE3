Soldering (and Crimping) the Electronic Circuits and Cables/Connectors

As well as having to laser cut solder masks for the SMT row_boards and led_board, and soldering them on a hot plate, there were many through-hole components and wires to hand-solder and quite a few connectors to painstakingly crimp onto cables in this phase.

This was several days of hard work, epsecially as I decided to test each board after soldering by plugging it into the prototype breadboard circuits and found problems, some vexing and difficult to diagnose, that had to be corrected on a number of the boards before I could continue to soldering or crimping the next pcb/cable.

Finally, there was the job of removing the store-bought USBC hub from its case, and retro-fitting it into the teensyExpression3 project. In reality that sub-task had been going on for a number of weeks as the USBC hub is instrumental to the device, providing the main USBC interface to the iPad, as well as being the power supply for the entire box. I spent much time figuring out how this particular USBC hub was wired, before desoldering and removing its original cable, soldering a new cable with a JST connector onto it, adding wires and a JST connector for powering the rPi, and testing it over and over again before even committing myself to do this project.

Mother Board

I discovered several problems in the motherboard after I created the PCB, soldered it, and started testing it. Specifically, in the photos below, the yellow and green wires on the top were added, due to the fact I had some lines wrong on the schematic, and the blue wire on the bottom was added to get the correct clock signal to the teensy 4.1's lcd touchscreen. Those fixes have been incorporated into the kicad files in this repo, but I continued to use the shown mother_board for this build.

Additionally (not shown) I totally messed up the wiring of the RCA jacks in the footprints for the RevD audio shield, and subsequently had to modify those traces on the PCB as well. Likewise, the corrections are present in the kicad files in this repo.

Row Boards

For soldering the SMT components to the row_boards, I made a solder mask by cutting it out of blue masking tape on my other larger cnc machine.

Unfortunately I did not take any photos of the solder-paste or hot-plate steps involved in soldering the SMT devices, but in the following image, you can see the results.

After the SMT components were soldered, I hand soldered the buttons and wires, and added the 6 pin JST connectors to four of the five row_boards.

As I finished soldering each row_board, I tested it with the prototype mother_board breadboard. I found several problem with traces being shorted and had to painstakingly exacto knife them to get them to work correctly.

Rotary Board

The rotary_board was hand-soldered, and fairly straight forward, though, once again, I found some problems in the traces that had to be corrected with an exacto knife.

LCD Connectors

Each LCD connector gets 3 resistors, two wires, and then an 8 pin cable with JST connectors crimped on.

LED Board

For the LED board, I also made a blue masking tape solder mask before adding the paste and heating them up to solder the LEDs and resistors. After that I cut the 8 wires for the cable, soldered them, and crimped on the JST8 connector.

I had never tried these particular 1206 SMT colored LEDs, and the green one was way too bright, so I had to replace the 220ohm resistor for it with a 1K resistor by hand soldering.

USB Stuff

There was a decent amount of work to solder up the various USB connectors and cables. and crimp JST connectors on them, starting with removing the resistors and adding JST connectors to the two USBC female breakout boards.

Then there were two of the the home made usba_connectors going to store-bought micro connectors and their tiny soldering points. Cut all the wires, strip em', tin the wires and connectors. Then solder amd weave the wires to the connectors, and hot glue the covers together.

Then the USBA breakout boards for the two external USB ports, one to a home made usba_connector, and the other to a crimped JST4 connector. The wires are hand-weaved. Later I started using little pieces of heatshrink to keep wire bundles neat, which is faster and simpler than weaving them.

The USBC Hub

The USBC Hub is a modification to a store-bought PCB. It needs it's own page describing in detail how it is modified. At the right in the photo below are two cables with JST connectors. The first, upper one, is a hand made USBC to JST6 cable for the power input. The second, lower one, for the external iPad or laptop to connect to, is soldered directly to the hub.

These are small wires soldered to tiny pads, so I applied prodiguous amounts of hot-glue, even though it not very pretty, to ensure that the solder joints and connectors were robust enough for practical use.

Finally, I drilled three 2mm mounting holes in the board and scratched out pads for the 5V power and soldered the red and black cable with its JST2 connector crimped on to that.

Testing

I wanted to make sure that before I started screwing PCBs into 3D printed plastic and hiding them inside the box, that everything was working correctly. So as I soldered each board, I tested it on my desk, which also meant that I had to write and evolve parts of the software to ensure that it was ok. It was messier than it sounds, but I got it nice and neat on my desk for the below photo.

Note that I had not yet soldered the big RCA and 1/4" audio connectors onto the motherboard in this photo.

After I was pretty certain that all the PCB's were basically working, and I had a start on the software, it was time to move to the next steps of 3D printing the box parts, laser-cutting the base from 1/4" plywood, and assembling the whole thing.
